# Dynasties — reference for the comparison / projection blurbs

The comparison and projection blurbs lean on named dynasties ("a Saban decade",
"a Georgia-style run") to make a percentile gap feel like a number of seasons.
This file is the lookup behind those phrases: what each dynasty actually produced,
across all ten rating stats, so the examples in the blurbs stay honest.

All figures are from the site's own staging data (`_staging_*`). Draft data begins
1967; AP-poll data begins 1936; consensus/unanimous All-America is full history.
Conference titles are noted from record but are not in the granular staging file.
"NCAA official" wins throughout (vacated seasons removed) — USC 2005 and the
Alabama, LSU, Ohio State, FSU, Notre Dame vacated seasons do **not** count here.

## The shapes

The dynasties don't all do the same thing. Six recurring shapes:

### 1. Everything at once — **Saban's Alabama (2009–20)**
The maximal case, and the one that reset the standard. ~12.6 wins a year at a
.910 clip, a national title every **two** years, **three** consensus
All-Americans a season, eight draft picks (nearly three in the first round) every
year, and it never left the poll — 190 of a possible ~190 top-10 weeks across the
run. That single 12-year stretch is most of what now separates the top six from
the field. Nothing else on this list matches it on more than one or two axes.
**Use for:** the bar itself; what "holding the standard" costs; the size of the
gap a Contender is really staring at.

### 2. The compressed peak — **Clemson (2015–20)**, **Carroll's USC (2002–08)**, **Osborne's Nebraska peak (1993–97)**
Five to seven years of near-perfect football — 13 wins a year, permanent top-5,
two or three titles — but *short*, and light on the accumulating stats.
Clemson's whole run produced only 8 consensus and 2 unanimous All-Americans;
Osborne's peak sent two players to the first round in five years. These runs move
championships, win rate and poll presence hard and fast; they barely touch a
program's all-time win total or its All-America count.
**Use for:** how a program proves a ceiling exists; why one great run isn't
enough to close a century-long ledger.

### 3. The NFL factory — **Miami "The U" (1983–94)**, **Carroll's USC**, **Georgia (2017–24)**, **LSU**
Talent volume as the signature. Georgia under Smart has the highest first-round
rate here (2.1 a year); Miami in its prime turned over a first-rounder a year on
a roster that also won four titles. High draft and first-round numbers, strong
poll presence — but not necessarily title-dense (LSU: three titles in 17 years)
and not necessarily heavy on consensus honors (Miami: 1.5 a year).
**Use for:** draft and first-round-pick gaps; the "talent is there, the finishing
isn't" programs.

### 4. Consistency over peaks — **Bowden's FSU (1987–2000)**, **modern Ohio State (2012–24)**
The plateau. FSU finished top-5 for **14 straight years** and parked 16 top-10
weeks a season, but won two titles seven years apart. Ohio State since 2012 wins
11.8 a year and is ranked essentially every week, with a title only every six or
seven seasons. Enormous poll-week and win totals; modest title density.
**Use for:** closing AP-poll-week and top-10-week gaps; the difference between
"always very good" and "champion".

### 5. The star system — **Switzer's Oklahoma (1973–87)**, **Osborne's full era (1971–97)**
Long runs built on individual dominance. Switzer's wishbone Sooners produced 27
consensus and 17 unanimous All-Americans in 15 years — an All-America rate the
title-denser modern dynasties don't reach — on a lower NFL-first-round count.
**Use for:** All-America (consensus and unanimous) gaps specifically; multi-decade
program-building rather than a single coach's peak.

### 6. The brand era — **Spurrier + Meyer's Florida (1990–2009)**, **LSU's three-title era**
Two decades, two or three titles, a permanent place in the poll — but a win% in
the .790s and real gaps between the peaks. The program is a fixture without ever
being *the* program for long.
**Use for:** National Powers and National Brands chasing the tier above; "a title
era, then a lean stretch, then another."

## Conversion cheat-sheet

Rough output of **one decade** of a genuine top-tier dynasty, for translating a
blurb's percentile gap into seasons:

| Stat | A dynasty decade produces… |
| --- | --- |
| Wins | ~110–125, at .875–.915 |
| National titles | 2–4 (Saban: 6; Osborne's peak: 3 in 5 years) |
| Conference titles | 6–9 |
| Consensus All-Americans | ~13–25 (star-system runs: 25+) |
| Unanimous All-Americans | ~4–12 |
| NFL draft picks | ~50–90 |
| First-round picks | ~10–25 (Saban: 33 in 12 years; Georgia: 2+/yr) |
| AP-poll weeks | ~150–165 (i.e. never unranked) |
| Top-10 weeks | ~130–190 |

**Rules of thumb for the blurbs:**

- A gap of **~40–60 consensus All-Americans** ≈ two dynasty decades of the star
  kind, or ~40 years of merely-very-good.
- A gap of **~200–250 AP-poll weeks** ≈ 13–15 unbroken ranked seasons — a
  Bowden-FSU or modern-Ohio-State plateau.
- A gap of **~6–8 national titles** is not a decade — it's a Saban-length run
  *plus* a second era on top, which is why only Georgia (already mid-run, trend
  up) gets it framed as realistic.
- A **first-round-pick** gap closes fastest: a Georgia- or Clemson-shaped run adds
  10–20 in under a decade.
- Programs with a thin **all-time win** base (Miami, FSU) can't close it with a
  dynasty at all — even Bowden's 14-year run only moved FSU's all-time total by
  ~150 wins. That gap needs decades of top-25 baseline, not a peak.
